Shilajit is sold as one thing. It isn't one thing. It's a tar-like resin that seeps from rock, and what ends up in the jar depends on which mountain it came from — which means two bottles with identical labels can hold meaningfully different chemistry.

That's not a marketing complaint. It's the central finding of the chemistry literature, and it changes how you should read every number on the package.

By the end you'll be able to find the declared extract amount on a label, check whether a lab report matches the jar you're holding, and turn a report's numbers into a daily figure.

<Callout tone="info">
This is **educational information**, not medical advice. The testing and labeling
patterns described are documented in the published literature, but whether shilajit
is appropriate for you is a conversation with your healthcare provider.
</Callout>

## Is shilajit safe? The short version

<Callout tone="accent">
**Its composition is genuinely unsettled.** A 2025 chemistry review concluded it "is not known with certainty" and that it differs substantially between regions. <EvidencePill level="Established" />

**Toxic metals are present, usually below the limits reviewers use, sometimes above.** A 2024 review found lead, arsenic, cadmium and mercury among roughly 65 metals reported in shilajit. <EvidencePill level="Moderate" />

**What a given jar contains takes a batch-matched lab report to answer.** The label can't. The rest of this post shows how to read one.
</Callout>

## What is shilajit, and why does every batch differ?

Shilajit forms over long periods in rock at high altitude. Whether it begins as decomposed plant matter, animal residue, or mineral secretion is still argued in the literature — a 2025 chemistry review notes its source "is not known" and that no complete chemical analysis has been published.

That matters more than it sounds. A multivitamin is manufactured to a specification. Shilajit is collected from geology. The same review found "many differences in the chemical compositions of Shilajit from various regions."

A 2026 animal study makes the variability concrete. Researchers tested several shilajit samples side by side and found their antioxidant activity spanned roughly a two-to-fourfold range, and that only two of the samples were notably rich in urolithin A and phenolic acids. Same substance by name. Different chemistry by sample.

<Source>
  Rizun N, et al., *Pharmaceuticals (Basel)* 2026 — [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42797531](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42797531/)
</Source>

So when a label says "pure Himalayan," it's naming a mountain range, not a formula.

<Source>
  [A Comprehensive Review on Shilajit: What We Know about Its Chemical Composition.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38133965/)
</Source>

## Does shilajit contain heavy metals?

Yes — and that alone isn't the alarming part. Rock-derived material carries rock chemistry. The question is how much, and whether anyone measured it.

A 2024 review in *Biological Trace Element Research* catalogued the metal profile across the published literature. It reported roughly 65 metals in shilajit, including the toxic ones: copper, aluminium, lead, arsenic, cadmium and mercury.

<Source>
  Hussain A, et al., *Biol Trace Elem Res* 2024 — [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38393486](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38393486/)
</Source>

The review compares shilajit against these figures, which it gives as the WHO and FDA recommended levels for heavy metals in herbal drugs:

| Metal | Level the review cites for herbal drugs |
|---|---|
| Cadmium | 0.20–0.30 ppm |
| Mercury | 1 ppm |
| Arsenic | 10 ppm |
| Lead | 10 ppm |
| Copper | 20 ppm |
| Zinc | 50 ppm |

Treat that table as the yardstick one review used, not as the legal limit for the jar you're holding. They are concentrations in herbal material, not daily intake limits. Which standard applies to a finished supplement depends on the country, and arsenic can be reported as total or inorganic. In the U.S., FDA's manufacturing rule requires each manufacturer to set its own limits on contaminants rather than supplying numbers for them, which is why a lab report should say what specification it was judged against.

<Source>
  21 CFR 111.70, specifications for dietary supplement manufacturing — [ecfr.gov](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-111/subpart-E/section-111.70)
</Source>

Against its yardstick, the review found reported shilajit levels "were found to be lower than the permissible limits set by WHO and FDA, except in few studies where exceeded levels were reported."

Hold both halves of that sentence. Most tested material cleared the limits. Some didn't. **Both can be true** — and from the outside of a sealed jar, you cannot tell which kind you bought.

Separately, a 2022 analysis ran commercial shilajit sold from India and Pakistan through three instrumental methods and detected lead, arsenic, mercury, chromium and nickel among roughly two dozen elements. <EvidencePill level="Moderate" />

<Source>
  Aldakheel RK, et al., *Biol Trace Elem Res* 2022 — [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34800280](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34800280/)
</Source>

The review's own conclusion is the practical rule: consuming shilajit "without knowing permissible levels of metals is not safe and could pose serious health problems." Without a measurement, the actual hazard in a given jar can't be assessed at all.

## What does "4,000 mg" on a shilajit label actually mean?

Often, considerably less than 4,000 mg of shilajit.

Botanical and resin ingredients are frequently sold as concentrated extracts described by a ratio. A 10:1 extract means 10 parts starting material were concentrated into 1 part extract. A front-of-bottle "4,000 mg" can therefore describe the *raw-material equivalent* — the amount it notionally started as — while the capsule contains 400 mg of actual extract.

Both numbers can be accurate. They describe different things, and the large one is the better marketing.

The way to resolve it is to read the Supplement Facts panel, which states the declared quantity of the ingredient per serving. If the front says 4,000 mg and the panel says 400 mg of a 10:1 extract, the two figures are consistent with each other — and the number to quote to a clinician is 400, alongside the serving size and ratio.

## Is shilajit bad for your kidneys or liver?

This is among the most-searched questions about shilajit. The direct toxicity data comes from animal studies that used different preparations, doses and designs, and their findings can't be carried over to every product.

A 2012 study gave rats a black shilajit preparation repeatedly over 91 days at 500, 2,500 or 5,000 mg/kg. Organ weights were normal, and tissue slides were normal apart from what the authors called negligible changes in the liver and intestine at the highest dose. The authors concluded it was safe for long-term use — a conclusion drawn from rats, one preparation and one laboratory. <EvidencePill level="Limited" />

<Source>
  Velmurugan C, et al., *Asian Pac J Trop Biomed* 2012 — [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23569899](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23569899/)
</Source>

A 2026 study in *Pharmaceuticals* gave several shilajit samples to rodents as a single dose and watched them for 14 days. There were no deaths, no visible signs of toxicity, and no notable changes in food intake, water intake or body weight, with a single-dose lethal threshold above 2,000 mg/kg.

Then they examined the tissue. Histopathology "revealed mild to moderate degenerative and necrotic changes in hepatic and renal tissues" — liver and kidney — though without liver fibrosis or pronounced kidney lesions. The authors' framing is careful: the findings "support satisfactory single acute oral tolerability under the study conditions but do not exclude tissue effects or establish their reversibility." They call for long-term safety studies of standardised resin. <EvidencePill level="Limited" />

<Source>
  Rizun N, et al., *Pharmaceuticals (Basel)* 2026 — [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42797531](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42797531/)
</Source>

Read both as what they are. Animals, not people. Specific preparations, not the product on your shelf. Neither shows that shilajit damages human kidneys, and neither establishes that it's safe for them over years of daily use.

If you have existing kidney or liver disease, that open question is the thing to raise with your clinician, rather than any claim on the packaging.

## Who should be most careful?

The contamination risk isn't evenly distributed, because lead isn't.

WHO's position is that "there is no level of exposure to lead that is known to be without harmful effects." Young children "may absorb up to 4–5 times as much lead as adults" from the same swallowed dose. And in pregnancy, lead already stored in bone "may be released into the blood during pregnancy and expose the fetus," with exposure linked to reduced fetal growth and preterm birth.

<Source>
  World Health Organization — Lead poisoning and health — [who.int](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/lead-poisoning-and-health)
</Source>

So at identical metal levels, an untested mineral resin is a different proposition for a pregnant reader or a child than for a healthy adult.

<Callout tone="warn">
**Raise it with a clinician before using shilajit if you are pregnant, breastfeeding, or considering it for a child** — an untested rock-derived resin is the wrong place to accept unknown lead exposure. The same applies if you have kidney or liver disease, given the open questions above, or a history of iron overload, since shilajit is mineral-rich by nature.
</Callout>

FDA's adverse-event database for foods and supplements (CAERS) adds a limited signal. A search on October 27, 2026 of the brand-name field for "shilajit" returned **6 reports**. Five carry a serious outcome category: two involved hospitalisation, one was classed as life-threatening, and two as other serious medical events. Two of the six describe allergic-type reactions — hives in both, with a swollen tongue and hypersensitivity in one. One report lists eight products taken together.

<Source>
  FDA CAERS, search of products.name_brand for "shilajit", data current to July 7, 2026 — [api.fda.gov](https://api.fda.gov/food/event.json?search=products.name_brand:%22shilajit%22)
</Source>

These are reports submitted to FDA, by consumers and health professionals voluntarily, and by the company named on a supplement's label, which must report serious adverse events it receives. Reporting is incomplete, and the records don't establish that shilajit caused an event or how often events occur. What they do show is that serious and allergic-type reactions have been reported, which is worth knowing before a first dose.

Recall history, from the same date's search of FDA enforcement reports, returned **two recalls naming shilajit**, and neither was for heavy metals. One, in 2026, involved under-processing that risked *Clostridium botulinum* growth, with health claims also listed in the recall; the other, in 2014, followed a supplier's organic powder recalled for possible *Salmonella*.

  FDA enforcement reports, search of product_description for "shilajit" — [api.fda.gov](https://api.fda.gov/food/enforcement.json?search=product_description:%22shilajit%22)

## How to read a shilajit lab report

"Third-party tested" on a product page leaves the important details unverified until you see the document: what was tested, by whom, on which lot, and with what results. Here's what to look for.

<Illustration caption="Anatomy of a certificate of analysis, using a fictional report">
  <ShilajitLabReport />
</Illustration>

- **A named laboratory with the right accreditation.** ISO/IEC 17025 is the usual standard for testing labs.
- **Numbers, not just verdicts.** "Complies" and "passed" can't be checked. A figure in ppm can.
- **The four metals by name** — lead, arsenic, cadmium, mercury — each with its own result and unit.
- **A batch or lot number that matches your jar.** Composition varies between batches; a report from a different lot describes a different material.
- **Finished product, not raw material.** Testing the incoming resin doesn't cover what processing added.
- **A stated reporting limit.** A result of "< 0.01" means below the report's limit, which may be a detection or a quantitation limit. It isn't zero.
- **A recent date.** A certificate from several years ago describes a batch you aren't holding.

Then do the arithmetic from Figure 3: ppm × grams per day = micrograms per day. That gives you and your clinician a daily figure to look at alongside your other exposure. A complete, matching report is one important piece of evidence; it doesn't settle whether the product suits you.

<Illustration caption="A checklist to take into a purchase, and into an appointment">

</Illustration>

Supplement quality questions rarely stay confined to one ingredient. Read the panel, ask for the batch document, convert to a daily figure. That habit separates a checkable product from a story, and it sits behind how we [assess evidence and product quality](/methodology/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Is shilajit legit?

As a traditional material with real chemistry, yes — it contains fulvic and humic substances and measurable marker compounds. As a standardised supplement, the evidence is thinner than the marketing. Its composition isn't fully characterised, it varies by source, and the human clinical literature is limited next to the claims made for it.

### Can you trust a "fulvic acid" percentage on the label?

Not on its own. Quantifying shilajit's markers — fulvic acid, hippuric acid, urolithin A — takes laboratory chromatography; a 2026 method paper developed HPLC, HPTLC and LC-MS procedures specifically because this measurement is non-trivial. A percentage with no assay method and no lab report behind it is a claim, not a measurement.

<Source>
  [Shilajit-analytical study to understand the phyto complex present in shilajit raw material, extract and resin by using hyphenated techniques.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41330163/)
</Source>

### Does shilajit raise testosterone?

There is one randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled human trial. Healthy men aged 45–55 took 250 mg of a purified shilajit preparation twice a day for 90 days, and their total testosterone, free testosterone and DHEAS rose significantly compared with placebo, while LH and FSH stayed stable. <EvidencePill level="Limited" />

<Source>
  Pandit S, et al., *Andrologia* 2016 — [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26395129](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26395129/)
</Source>

The limits matter as much as the result. It was one purified preparation, in one age group of healthy men, over three months, and it measured hormone levels rather than symptoms or long-term safety. It doesn't tell you what a different commercial resin would do. Animal work points the same way with the same caveat: in the 2026 rodent study, an exploratory follow-up of the two samples with the highest free testosterone found levels roughly 2.5-fold (total) and 3.5-fold (free) above controls — a finding that varied by sample.

### Is shilajit FDA approved?

No, and no supplement is. Dietary supplements don't undergo FDA pre-market approval for safety or effectiveness; manufacturers are responsible for their own purity specifications. "FDA registered facility" describes a registration, not an inspection verdict or an endorsement of the product.

### What's the single most useful thing to check?

The Supplement Facts panel and a batch-matched lab report with numeric metal results. Between them they answer more about the jar than anything else on the package.
