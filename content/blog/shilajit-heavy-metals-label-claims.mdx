Shilajit is sold as one thing. It isn't one thing. It's a tar-like resin that seeps from rock, and what ends up in the jar depends on which mountain it came from — which means two bottles with identical labels can hold meaningfully different chemistry.

That's not a marketing complaint. It's the central finding of the chemistry literature, and it changes how you should read every number on the package.

<Callout tone="info">
This is **educational information**, not medical advice. The testing and labeling
patterns described are documented in the published literature, but whether shilajit
is appropriate for you is a conversation with your healthcare provider.
</Callout>

## Is shilajit safe? The short version

<Callout tone="accent">
**Shilajit's composition is genuinely unsettled.** A 2025 review of the chemistry concluded its composition "is not known with certainty," because no complete chemical analysis has ever been done — and that compositions differ substantially between regions. <EvidencePill level="Established" />

**Toxic metals are present, usually below limits, sometimes above.** A 2024 review counted roughly 65 metals in shilajit, lead, arsenic, cadmium and mercury among them. Reported levels sat mostly under the WHO and FDA ceilings for herbal products — "except in few studies where exceeded levels were reported." <EvidencePill level="Moderate" />

**The authors' own conclusion is the practical one:** consuming shilajit without knowing its metal levels "is not safe and could pose serious health problems."

**What this means for you:** the deciding factor isn't the brand story or the milligram number on the front. It's whether a batch-specific lab report exists, and whether you can read it. The rest of this post is how to do that.
</Callout>

## What is shilajit, and why does every batch differ?

Shilajit forms over long periods in rock at high altitude. Whether it begins as decomposed plant matter, animal residue, or mineral secretion is still argued in the literature — a 2025 chemistry review notes its source "is not known" and that no complete chemical analysis has been published.

That matters more than it sounds. A multivitamin is manufactured to a specification. Shilajit is collected from geology. The same review found "many differences in the chemical compositions of Shilajit from various regions."

A 2026 animal study makes the variability concrete. Researchers tested several shilajit samples side by side and found their antioxidant activity spanned roughly a two-to-fourfold range, and that only two of the samples were notably rich in urolithin A and phenolic acids. Same substance by name. Different chemistry by sample.

So when a label says "pure Himalayan," it's naming a mountain range, not a formula.

**Source:** [A Comprehensive Review on Shilajit: What We Know about Its Chemical Composition.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38133965/)

## Does shilajit contain heavy metals?

Yes — and that alone isn't the alarming part. Rock-derived material carries rock chemistry. The question is how much, and whether anyone measured it.

A 2024 review in *Biological Trace Element Research* catalogued the metal profile across the published literature. It reported roughly 65 metals in shilajit, including the toxic ones: copper, aluminium, lead, arsenic, cadmium and mercury.

<Source>
  Hussain A, et al., *Biol Trace Elem Res* 2024 — [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38393486](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38393486/)
</Source>

The same review lists the ceilings WHO and FDA apply to herbal products. These are the numbers a lab report gets measured against:

| Metal | Permissible limit in herbal products |
|---|---|
| Cadmium | 0.20–0.30 ppm |
| Mercury | 1 ppm |
| Arsenic | 10 ppm |
| Lead | 10 ppm |
| Copper | 20 ppm |
| Zinc | 50 ppm |

Measured against those, the review found reported shilajit levels "were found to be lower than the permissible limits set by WHO and FDA, except in few studies where exceeded levels were reported."

Hold both halves of that sentence. Most tested material cleared the limits. Some didn't. **Both can be true** — and from the outside of a sealed jar, you cannot tell which kind you bought.

Separately, a 2022 analysis ran commercial shilajit sold from India and Pakistan through three instrumental methods and detected lead, arsenic, mercury, chromium and nickel among roughly two dozen elements. <EvidencePill level="Moderate" />

<Source>
  Aldakheel RK, et al., *Biol Trace Elem Res* 2022 — [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34800280](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34800280/)
</Source>

This is where the review's own conclusion earns repeating, because it's the practical rule: consuming shilajit "without knowing permissible levels of metals is not safe and could pose serious health problems." The named risk isn't the resin. It's the not knowing.

## What does "4,000 mg" on a shilajit label actually mean?

Often, considerably less than 4,000 mg of shilajit.

Botanical and resin ingredients are frequently sold as concentrated extracts described by a ratio. A 10:1 extract means 10 parts starting material were concentrated into 1 part extract. A front-of-bottle "4,000 mg" can therefore describe the *raw-material equivalent* — the amount it notionally started as — while the capsule contains 400 mg of actual extract.

Both numbers are real. They describe different things. The large one is the better marketing.

The way to resolve it is to ignore the front of the bottle entirely and read the Supplement Facts panel, which states the quantity of the ingredient actually present per serving. If the front says 4,000 mg and the panel says 400 mg of a 10:1 extract, nothing is being hidden — but the number you'd quote to a clinician is 400.

## Is shilajit bad for your kidneys or liver?

This is among the most-searched questions about shilajit, and the honest answer is that the only direct toxicity data is recent, in animals, and mixed in a specific way.

A 2026 study in *Pharmaceuticals* gave shilajit to rodents and looked at acute toxicity. The headline result was reassuring: no deaths, no visible signs of toxicity, and no notable changes in food intake, water intake or body weight across 14 days, with a single-dose lethal threshold above 2,000 mg/kg.

Then they examined the tissue. Histopathology "revealed mild to moderate degenerative and necrotic changes in hepatic and renal tissues" — liver and kidney — though without liver fibrosis or pronounced kidney lesions. The authors' framing is careful and worth quoting: the findings "support satisfactory single acute oral tolerability under the study conditions but do not exclude tissue effects or establish their reversibility." They close by calling for long-term safety studies. <EvidencePill level="Limited" />

<Source>
  Rizun N, et al., *Pharmaceuticals (Basel)* 2026 — [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42797531](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42797531/)
</Source>

Read that as what it is. Animals, not people. One large dose, not months of daily use. Fourteen days, not years.

It does not show that shilajit damages human kidneys. It does not clear it either. What it shows is tissue-level change that nobody has followed up — in a supplement people take daily, for years.

If you have existing kidney or liver disease, that unresolved gap is the thing to raise with your clinician, rather than any claim on the packaging.

## Who should be most careful?

The contamination risk isn't evenly distributed, because lead isn't.

WHO's position is that "there is no level of exposure to lead that is known to be without harmful effects." Young children "may absorb up to 4–5 times as much lead as adults" from the same swallowed dose. And in pregnancy, lead already stored in bone "may be released into the blood during pregnancy and expose the fetus," with exposure linked to reduced fetal growth and preterm birth.

<Source>
  World Health Organization — Lead poisoning and health — [who.int](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/lead-poisoning-and-health)
</Source>

So at identical metal levels, an untested mineral resin is a different proposition for a pregnant reader or a child than for a healthy adult.

<Callout tone="warn">
**Raise it with a clinician before using shilajit if you are pregnant, breastfeeding, or considering it for a child** — an untested rock-derived resin is the wrong place to accept unknown lead exposure. The same applies if you have kidney or liver disease, given the unresolved tissue findings above, or a history of iron overload, since shilajit is mineral-rich by nature.
</Callout>

FDA's own records add a modest but real signal. Its adverse-event system for foods and supplements holds **6 reports naming shilajit**, of which 3 carried serious outcomes — two involving hospitalisation and one classed as life-threatening. The reported reactions cluster noticeably around allergic-type effects: hives, swollen tongue, skin peeling, hypersensitivity.

<Source>
  FDA CAERS, adverse event reports naming shilajit — [api.fda.gov](https://api.fda.gov/food/event.json?search=products.name_brand:%22shilajit%22)
</Source>

These are voluntary reports. They establish that something was reported, never that shilajit caused it. Six reports is a small number against a product selling at this volume — but an allergic pattern is worth knowing before a first dose.

Recall history is thinner still, and worth stating plainly because it cuts against the scarier version of this story: only **two FDA food recalls on record name shilajit**, and neither was for heavy metals. One, in 2026, involved under-processing that risked *Clostridium botulinum* growth alongside an unlawful health claim; the other, in 2014, followed a supplier's contaminated organic powder.

## How to read a shilajit lab report

A product page that says "third-party tested" without producing a document has told you nothing. Here's what turns the phrase into evidence.

- **A named, accredited laboratory.** An ISO 17025 accreditation is the ordinary mark.
- **Numbers, not verdicts.** "Complies" and "passed" can't be checked. A figure in ppm can.
- **The four metals by name** — lead, arsenic, cadmium, mercury — each with its own result.
- **A batch or lot number that matches your jar.** Composition varies between batches; a report from a different lot describes a different material.
- **Finished product, not raw material.** Testing the incoming resin doesn't cover what processing added.
- **A recent date.** A certificate from several years ago describes a batch you aren't holding.

Then do the arithmetic from the diagram above: ppm × grams per day = micrograms per day. That single number is what you can actually compare — against published ceilings, and against the rest of your diet.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is shilajit legit?

As a traditional material with real chemistry, yes — it contains fulvic and humic substances and measurable marker compounds. As a standardised supplement, the evidence is thinner than the marketing. Its composition isn't fully characterised, it varies by source, and the human clinical literature stays limited next to the claims made for it.

### Can you trust a "fulvic acid" percentage on the label?

Not on its own. Quantifying shilajit's markers — fulvic acid, hippuric acid, urolithin A — takes laboratory chromatography; a 2026 method paper developed HPLC, HPTLC and LC-MS procedures specifically because this measurement is non-trivial. A percentage with no assay method and no lab report behind it is a claim, not a measurement.

**Source:** [Shilajit-analytical study to understand the phyto complex present in shilajit raw material, extract and resin by using hyphenated techniques.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41330163/)

### Does shilajit raise testosterone?

Early research in animals suggests an effect for some samples, and the detail matters. In the 2026 rodent study, an exploratory follow-up of the two samples with the highest free testosterone found total and free testosterone roughly 2.5-fold and 3.5-fold above controls. That's two samples, in rats, in an exploratory analysis — not a human dosing result, and a finding that varied by sample rather than applying to shilajit generally. <EvidencePill level="Limited" />

### Is shilajit FDA approved?

No, and no supplement is. Dietary supplements don't undergo FDA pre-market approval for safety or effectiveness; manufacturers are responsible for their own purity specifications. "FDA registered facility" describes a notification, not an inspection verdict or an endorsement of the product.

### What's the single most useful thing to check?

The Supplement Facts panel and a batch-matched lab report with numeric metal results. Those two documents outrank every other signal on the package.
